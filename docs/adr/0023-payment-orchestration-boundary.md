# The sidecar is a payment gateway, not a payment aggregator

ADR-0021 asks whether the sidecar is an Electronic Commerce Operator under GST and answers no, because it never collects consideration. That is one regulator. A payment processor's compliance team asks a different question first, and no ADR had asked it: under RBI's Payment Aggregator / Payment Gateway framework, does a box that calls `make-link`, `block` and `refund` on the Merchant's behalf and receives the Provider's webhooks need authorisation to operate a payment system?

The framework draws its line on fund handling, and it draws it explicitly. A Payment Aggregator receives payments from customers, pools them, and transfers them on to merchants — any non-bank doing that requires RBI authorisation under the Payment and Settlement Systems Act. A Payment Gateway provides the technology infrastructure that routes and processes an online payment without any involvement in handling funds; PGs are treated as "technology providers" or "outsourcing partners" of the banks and non-banks they serve, and entities functioning as gateways do not require RBI authorisation (RBI, *Guidelines on Regulation of Payment Aggregators and Payment Gateways*, 17 March 2020, as amended, and the successor Master Direction).

The sidecar sits on the gateway side of that line by construction, not by luck: the payer's counterparty is the Merchant's own Provider account (SPEC §2), settlement lands there, and no primitive in the Provider trait (`make-link` / `check-status` / `cancel` / `refund` / optional `block` / `capture-block` / `release-block`, ADR-0013) moves money into or through anything OpenStore controls. There is nothing to pool. This is the same structural property ADR-0007 produced for a different reason and ADR-0021 relies on for a third.

Said the way ADR-0021 says it: this is a read, not a ruling. Two things keep it from being free. First, the PG classification is not an exemption from everything — it carries the baseline technology and security expectations the guidelines set for gateways (storage and transmission of payment data, security audit, incident reporting), and those are obligations on this codebase, not on the Merchant. Second, an acquirer's own contract routinely pushes merchant due-diligence and onboarding obligations onto whoever integrates on the merchant's behalf, which is a contractual exposure the regulatory classification does not touch.

What is fixed here is the property that makes the read hold, and it is the same sentence ADR-0021 ends on for the same reason: no version of this system in which funds settle anywhere but the Merchant's own account may ship without reopening this ADR, because pooling is precisely the fact that converts a gateway into an aggregator. A hosted or multi-tenant design (`PLAN-distribution.md` D5, and the K1 fork in `docs/REVIEW-findings.md`) is the likely place that pressure arrives.

Consequences: none to the code — this documents a boundary the architecture already holds, and the practical instruction is to keep it holding: the sidecar initiates requests the Merchant's own Provider credentials authorise, and never proxies, pools, or transforms payment data in a way that reads as operating the payment flow. The baseline gateway security expectations are folded into the guardrails SPEC §12 already imposes rather than tracked separately. Cross-linked from SPEC §2 and ADR-0021, and carried into the counsel engagement (`docs/COUNSEL-BRIEF.md`) as the question to confirm rather than the question to research — the reviewer finding that prompted this (PSP-1, "RBI's framework doesn't hinge on fund custody alone") is, on the published guidelines, wrong about the licensing test, and the ADR is written so that a counsel opinion can contradict it cheaply if it turns out to be wrong about that.
