# Reach is earned permissionlessly; native in-agent completion is not bought with authority

A correct sidecar nobody's agent can see is not a product. The reach problem is real and it is not solved by more protocol conformance: agentic discovery today is a small set of bilateral integrations, and a merchant behind OpenStore can be perfectly UCP-conformant and still receive zero agent traffic.

There are two kinds of door, and we treat them differently.

**Permissionless doors are the whole v-next strategy.** Product feeds in Google Merchant Center attribute names and schema.org `Product`/`ProductGroup`/`Offer` markup are ingested by shopping and AI surfaces without anyone's approval; an MCP server added by URL is reachable by every MCP client without an application, a tier review, or a partner agreement. Both are already implied by SPEC §3 and the 9-door trait. Finishing them is engineering we control end to end, and they are the doors where ADR-0012's "any Buyer Agent, no pre-registration" stops being a principle and starts being distribution.

**Gated doors are business development wearing a protocol costume, and they have a price.** Native in-agent completion on the large assistant surfaces runs on delegated payment credentials — a token the Consumer issues once that an agent later spends without them present. That is precisely the authority ADR-0008 removed from agents, and ADR-0013 refuses it. We can be present on those surfaces as redirect-completion, which converts worse. We accept the worse conversion. We do not accept the token. (Redirect completion is not itself a protocol violation — UCP defines buyer escalation with a `continue_url` and an embedded binding for it, so what we decline is a trust tier, not conformance. SPEC §9's badge wording overstates this and is corrected per `docs/REVIEW-findings.md` R1.)

Two things make that less of a sacrifice than it reads as.

First, the deviation is narrower than "we are not conformant": everything up to payment is native, and only the spend leaves the agent. A buyer taps once, on the merchant's own domain, for a `cart_hash` they can see.

Second, the Indian picture is a band rather than a moat, and it is moving. Today's facts still favour us at the top of the range: card-not-present spend attracts an additional-factor requirement, stored card credentials are subject to tokenisation rules, and a UPI PIN is by construction entered by the payer in the payer's own PSP app. But NPCI is actively building the thing that relaxes this — a Unified Agent Protocol letting an agent spend without per-transaction approval, layered on UPI Circle delegation and Reserve Pay fund blocking. It was scheduled to launch at GFF 2026 on 12 September 2026 and was pulled the night before, held pending clearance from the RBI while NPCI works through user protection, the liability question when an agent goes rogue, and governance standards; NPCI is expected to seek approval again within months (Business Standard, 11 September 2026). Correcting an earlier revision of this paragraph, which said UAP "appeared only as work its board acknowledged in the abstract": that understated it. The protocol substantially exists, its shape is known — verified agents transacting within pre-authorised, user-set spending limits, built on UPI Circle delegation — and the blocker is a regulator's approval rather than an absent specification. Its published limits and authentication model are still not out, so it remains undesignable-against in detail, but it is a thing arriving on a regulatory clock, not a rumour.

That the RBI is the gate is itself informative, and it cuts our way. The Reserve Bank of India (Authentication mechanisms for digital payment transactions) Directions, 2025 — issued 25 September 2025, compliance required by 1 April 2026 — require every domestic digital payment to be authenticated by two factors from separate categories, **dynamically linked to the specific transaction**, with static credentials explicitly insufficient (cross-border card-not-present follows by 1 October 2026). That is a stronger and more precise version of the "additional-factor requirement" this ADR gestured at above, it applies across rails rather than to cards alone, and it is the standard any agentic delegation has to be reconciled with before it ships in India. What the existing caps suggest is the shape: delegation sanctioned for low-value, high-frequency spend, with per-transaction authentication still expected above it. The caps, with their sources, because a remembered figure here is worth nothing (`docs/EXPANSION.md` §1 citation rule): a Reserve Pay block is **a maximum of ₹10,000 and up to 90 days** — NPCI/UPI/OC-228/2025-26, 8 October 2025, which enriches the feature introduced by NPCI/UPI/OC-200/2024-25, 31 July 2024 — and UPI Circle full delegation is around ₹15,000 a month, both possibly revised for agentic use. The same circular is more pointed than the cap alone: Reserve Pay "shall be enabled only for online verified merchants with low ticket and high frequency transactions," which is the acquirer's selection rule, not just a per-block ceiling. The band this ADR argues for is therefore drawn by NPCI in NPCI's own words, and it is narrower than the number alone suggests.

So the honest claim is narrower than "our tap is the compliant path": for a grocery reorder, delegation is coming and is the better experience, and we will lose that trade. For a basket worth more than a monthly delegation cap — which is most of what a D2C store sells — a per-spend tap bound to the cart remains what the rules expect and what a merchant wants when the order is disputed. We compete in the second band and concede the first, and a legal opinion should confirm where the line actually falls before either claim is made to a counterparty.

What GFF 2026 did produce is a statement of architecture from the rail operator, and it is close to a restatement of ours. NPCI's non-executive chairman, Ajay Kumar Choudhary, put the boundary this way: "Decision making and execution must remain separate", and "AI may recommend, but authentication and final settlement must follow deterministic auditable rules" — agents identify intent, an authorizer verifies identity and mandates, settlement stays deterministic, because "AI is adaptive and its output is probabilistic. Payment infrastructure has to stay deterministic, auditable and final." He added that NPCI "is also examining the protocols that may be required to identify and authorise digital agents within the UPI ecosystem while preserving interoperability, auditability and settlement finality", without timeline or limits.

That is the three-way split this sidecar already implements: the Buyer Agent proposes, the Gate is the deterministic authorizer, and the Transcript plus the sealed receipt are the auditable artifact. It is worth saying in those words rather than in ours — "deterministic, auditable authorisation on the merchant side" is a sentence a counterparty and a regulator both already parse, where "proof-carrying commerce" is one neither does. It is positioning, not a design change, and it does not make our tap the compliant path by itself; the band in the paragraphs above is unchanged and still wants a legal opinion.

One thing UAP changes in our favour: if agent-side delegation arrives on UPI rails, the delegation lives in the bank and NPCI stack with defined caps, revocation and recourse — not in a credential a foreign commerce platform issues and holds. That is a different object from a Shop Pay or ACP token, and it reaches us the way every instrument does, through the Payment Provider trait declaring its methods (ADR-0013), without an agent ever holding spending authority we granted. Supporting UAP when it ships should therefore be a provider adapter rather than a reversal of ADR-0008 — but that is a claim about a spec we have not written yet, not one the current spec supports: the Gate's first check is `human-authority-present` and SPEC §12 refuses `confirm` without a fresh tap regardless, so a bank-held mandate is refused by every path we have. Making the claim true costs one closed set, added now: `authority ∈ {tap, mandate}`, with `tap` the only member v1 accepts and `mandate` defined, registered, recorded in the Transcript, and refused with a named code (`docs/REVIEW-findings.md` F3). Watch UAP; do not build for it while its rules are unpublished — but leave the seam where the adapter would land, because a closed set that never anticipated a second member is how a configuration becomes an architecture change.

Consequences: `PLAN-distribution.md` carries the permissionless doors as phase-two work, sequenced after the install gate (`PLAN.md` step 7), because a surface that sends agents to a store nobody can install wastes the only first impression available. Gated surfaces are pursued only with live merchants behind us, and only ever as redirect-completion. Any future proposal to accept a delegated credential — even as a per-merchant opt-in — reopens ADR-0008 and ADR-0013 together and needs its own ADR and an Indian regulatory opinion, not a configuration flag. Two follow-ons fall out of this revision: the authority closed set above needs its own ADR before S3 freezes the Gate's check order, since it touches the boundary ADR-0008 drew; and SPEC §9's conformance-badge wording is corrected to describe escalation-only completion rather than non-conformance, with the embedded-checkout binding specified in Sidecar S6 so the tap renders inside the agent surface instead of as a browser bounce.
